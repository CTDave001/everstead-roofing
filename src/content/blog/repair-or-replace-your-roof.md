This is the question almost every homeowner opens with, and it's the one where the incentives are least aligned. A replacement is worth considerably more to a contractor than a repair. So it's worth knowing how to read the situation yourself.

## Signals that point to repair

A repair is usually the right call when the problem is **localized and the rest of the roof is sound**:

- A single leak with an identifiable source — a failed pipe boot, a lifted shingle, damaged flashing around a chimney or skylight
- Storm damage confined to one slope or one area
- The roof is comfortably inside its expected life, and the surrounding shingles are still flexible with their granules intact

Flashing is worth calling out specifically. A surprising share of leaks people assume are shingle failures are actually flashing failures — at chimneys, valleys, and sidewalls. Flashing can be replaced without touching the field of the roof, and it costs a fraction of a replacement.

## Signals that point to replacement

Replacement starts making sense when the problem is **systemic rather than local**:

- **Age.** Most asphalt roofs are specified for 20 to 30 years. Past 20, repairs start becoming a series of payments toward an outcome you'll reach anyway.
- **Widespread granule loss.** Check your gutters. Heavy granule accumulation means the shingle surface is breaking down across the whole roof, not in one spot.
- **Multiple leaks in separate locations.** One leak is an event. Three leaks in three places is a pattern, and patching each one individually is rarely the cheaper path.
- **Curling, cupping, or brittleness.** Shingles that curl at the edges or crack when flexed have lost their oils. That is not repairable.
- **Sagging.** A visible dip in the roofline suggests the decking underneath has been compromised. That's structural and it needs opening up.

## The questions worth asking

Whatever recommendation you get, these three questions will tell you a great deal about who you're dealing with:

**"Can you show me?"** Photographs of the specific problem areas. Any contractor who has been on your roof should have them, and should be glad to walk you through them.

**"What happens if I repair it instead?"** A straight answer sounds like *"you'll likely get three to five more years, and here's what will fail next."* A vague answer, or immediate pressure toward replacement, is informative in itself.

**"What's the condition of the decking?"** If they haven't looked, or can't say, they haven't inspected thoroughly enough to be recommending anything.

## A note on insurance

If the damage came from a storm, the repair-versus-replace question may be partly your insurer's to answer. Document everything before any work happens — photographs, dates, and the inspection report. Adjusters work from evidence, and evidence gathered after the fact is worth much less.
