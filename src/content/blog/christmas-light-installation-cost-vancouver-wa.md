Almost nobody publishes holiday lighting prices. You call, you get a "we'd need to come out and look at it," and three days later a number arrives with no explanation of how it was reached.

Here is the number, and here is how it is reached.

## The short answer

**Most residential Christmas light installations around Vancouver run $400 to $1,500, fully installed.**

That should cover the lights themselves, the installation, outdoor-rated cords, a timer, and taking everything back down in January. If a quote you are comparing does not include all five of those, it is not the same product.

Small single-story homes with a simple roofline sit at the bottom of that range. Large two-story homes with multiple gables, wrapped columns, and a few trees sit at the top, and genuinely elaborate displays can run past it.

## What actually drives the price

Square footage is not the driver people assume it is. A 3,000 sq ft ranch with one long, clean roofline is cheaper to light than a 1,800 sq ft home with four gables and a dormer. What matters is:

### Linear feet of roofline

This is the biggest single factor. Installers price the run, not the house. Every foot of roofline is material plus the time to clip it.

### Height and access

Single-story work is faster and safer than a steep two-story pitch. The second story is not twice the price, but it is meaningfully more, because the setup — ladder positioning, fall protection, moving everything around the house — is where the time goes.

### Peaks, valleys, and detail work

Long horizontal runs are the most light per dollar you will ever buy. Every peak means a cut, a measurement, and a transition. Outlining individual windows is beautiful and is priced accordingly.

### Trees, columns, and railings

Wrapping is slow. A single well-wrapped trunk can take as long as a substantial stretch of roofline. It is worth it visually — just know that it is priced separately for a reason.

### Whether removal is included

Some quotes are low because takedown is a separate January invoice. Ask directly.

## The quotes worth walking away from

**Anyone who wants to staple or nail into your roof.** This is the one that matters. Holiday lights should be hung on clips designed for the purpose — no holes, no nails, no staples. Fasteners driven through shingles or flashing create entry points for water, and around here that water has from October to May to find its way in. A $200 saving in November is not worth a leak diagnosis in February.

We repair those roofs the rest of the year. The damage is not hypothetical.

**Anyone who will not put the removal date in writing.** Lights left up into spring are a sign of a company that overbooked, and a strand that spends six months in UV and rain is a strand you are replacing.

**Retail-strand pricing.** If a quote is dramatically below everyone else's, check what is being hung. Big-box consumer strands are not built for a full season of Pacific Northwest weather, and the failure rate shows up right around the time you most want them working.

## Why booking early is cheaper than it looks

Holiday lighting is one of the few trades where the calendar is a hard physical limit. There are a finite number of dry, workable days between early October and Christmas, and every installer in Clark County is drawing from the same pool of them.

The practical effect: dates are assigned in the order deposits arrive. By the week after Thanksgiving — which is when most people start calling — what is left is whatever nobody else wanted. Booking in September or October does not usually buy you a discount, but it buys you the date you actually want, which for most people is worth more.
